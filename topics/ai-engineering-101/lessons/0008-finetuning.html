Finetuning

Changing the model itself: when it's worth it, why it eats memory, and how LoRA makes it affordable.

Lesson 8 · Previews Chapter 7 · about 25 min

Your win today You'll be able to look at a failing feature and say whether it needs RAG (an information failure) or finetuning (a behavior failure). You'll also be able to estimate on a napkin whether a model fits on a GPU for serving, full finetuning or LoRA.

Warm-up · spaced review

1 · What finetuning is

Prompting, RAG and agents all adapt a model through its input. Finetuning adapts it by training it further, so its weights change. It's a form of transfer learning: what the model learned from abundant data (predicting text) transfers to a task with little data. Training a legal Q&A model from scratch might need millions of examples, while finetuning a good base model might need a few hundred. Ch7 · Finetuning Overview

You've met the kinds of finetuning already, and the Lesson 3 question still sorts them. Who writes the answer? Nobody: continued pre-training on raw domain text, such as legal documents. A human writes it: supervised finetuning on (instruction, response) pairs. A human only picks the better of two: preference finetuning. Huyen notes that in practice finetuning is used most often to make a model follow a specific style or format. Ch7 · Finetuning Overview

2 · Facts or form?

Finetuning costs more than prompting in data, hardware and ML skill, and the cost doesn't end at launch. You have to serve the finetuned model, and when a better base model comes out (often), you have to decide whether to redo the work. Finetuning for one task can also degrade others. Huyen's warning from practitioners is that “prompting doesn't work” usually means the prompt experiments were minimal and unsystematic. Ch7 · Reasons Not to Finetune

Specialist models don't automatically win either. BloombergGPT (50B parameters, an estimated $1.3–2.6M in compute) launched in March 2023. In the same month GPT-4 came out, and with zero-shot prompting it beat BloombergGPT on financial benchmarks. Ch7 · Finetuning Domain-Specific Tasks

So once you've done prompting well, the choice between RAG and finetuning depends on how the model fails. Ch7 · Finetuning and RAG

Huyen's suggested order: (0) build your eval pipeline first, then (1) prompt systematically, (2) add examples (1 to 50), (3) add simple retrieval such as BM25 if facts are missing, (4) go further based on the failure: advanced RAG for information failures, finetuning for behavior ones, (5) or both. If the model has both kinds of failure, start with RAG, because it's easier. Ch7 · Finetuning and RAG

3 · Why finetuning eats memory

The rule: memory = (number of values) × (bytes per value). A model stored in 16 bits takes 2 bytes per parameter. Serving needs the weights plus about 20% for activations and the KV cache. Training additionally keeps three numbers for every parameter you train: its gradient and Adam's two optimizer states. Ch7 · Memory Math

Think of packing for a trip. Every parameter is an item, and the number format sets how big each item is. Training means that every item you want to change brings three more items with it. There are two ways to fit in the suitcase: make each item smaller, or change fewer items.

Inference      ≈ params × bytes × 1.2
               13B × 2 bytes = 26 GB weights → ≈ 31 GB

Full finetune  ≈ weights + trainable params × 3 × 2 bytes   (+ activations)
               7B: 14 GB weights + 7B × 6 bytes = 42 GB → 56 GB
               Consumer GPUs have 12–24 GB.

Lever 1: fewer bytes per value (quantization)

Formats: FP32 = 4 bytes, FP16 and BF16 = 2, INT8 = 1, INT4 = ½. Converting to fewer bits is called quantization, and it's cheap and highly effective. Serving in 16, 8 or even 4 bits is now standard, usually by quantizing a finished model (post-training quantization). Training is more sensitive, so it's done in higher or mixed precision. One trap: BF16 and FP16 are both 16 bits but split them differently. Llama 2 shipped in BF16, and teams that loaded it as FP16 got noticeably worse output. Ch7 · Numerical Representations; Quantization

Lever 2: fewer trainable parameters (PEFT)

Parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT) freezes almost all the weights and trains a tiny number of new ones. The first adapters (Houlsby et al., 2019) came within 0.4% of full finetuning while training 3% of the parameters, but they added layers, and so added latency. PEFT is also sample-efficient: a few thousand examples can be enough, where full finetuning wants far more. Ch7 · Parameter-Efficient Finetuning

4 · LoRA, the default

LoRA (low-rank adaptation) is by far the most-used PEFT method. For a weight matrix W, it keeps W frozen and learns two thin matrices, A (n × r) and B (r × m), whose product has the same shape as W. Only A and B train. Afterwards, A × B can be added into W, so the finetuned model is no slower. The rank r is the knob: 4 to 64 is usually enough, and larger values often don't help. Ch7 · LoRA

Because the adapters are small and separate, one base model can serve many of them (multi-LoRA serving). For 100 customers with one 4096 × 4096 matrix each, storing 100 merged matrices takes 1.68B parameters, while one shared W plus 100 adapters takes 23.3M. Switching customers means loading only an adapter. Apple serves several adapters on one 3B on-device model this way. The catch: LoRA usually doesn't reach full-finetuning quality. Ch7 · Serving LoRA adapters

QLoRA combines both levers: the frozen weights are stored in 4 bits (the NF4 format) and LoRA adapters are trained on top. That let Dettmers et al. finetune a 65B model on a single 48 GB GPU. Ch7 · Quantized LoRA

What did you just see? (open after the challenges)
  • Serving 13B needs about 31 GB in 16 bits. INT8 (about 16 GB) or 4-bit fits. Quantizing for inference is routine.
  • Full finetuning 7B needs 56 GB, three-quarters of it gradients and optimizer states. LoRA cuts the trainable parameters, and 16-bit weights plus a small adapter come to about 14 GB.
  • 65B is 130 GB of weights in 16 bits, so LoRA alone isn't enough. You also need 4-bit weights: about 33 GB plus the adapter. That's QLoRA.

6 · If you do finetune

The training itself is the easy part. Frameworks and APIs handle it, and the hard parts are deciding to finetune, getting data (Lesson 9) and maintaining the result. Ch7 · Finetuning Tactics

DecisionHuyen's default
MethodStart with LoRA; try full finetuning later. With only a few hundred examples, full finetuning may not beat LoRA.
WhereA finetuning API is quickest but limits the models and knobs. Frameworks (PEFT, Axolotl, unsloth, LLaMA-Factory) give control, on your own GPUs.
Learning rateThe step size. A jumpy loss curve means it's too big; slow and flat means it's too small.
EpochsIf training loss falls while validation loss rises, the model is overfitting: train fewer epochs.
Many tasksTraining tasks one after another risks catastrophic forgetting. Train them together, or separately and then merge the models.
